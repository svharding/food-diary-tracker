      How to read your results

      The reference values

      • EAR (Estimated Average Requirement): meets the needs of half of healthy people in your group.
      • RDA (Recommended Dietary Allowance): meets the needs of nearly all (97–98%) healthy people in your group.
      • AI (Adequate Intake): used when there is not enough evidence to set an RDA. If you are at or above the AI, your intake is probably adequate. If you are below it, no conclusion can be drawn.
      • UL (Tolerable Upper Intake Level): the highest usual intake unlikely to cause harm. For some nutrients (magnesium, folic acid, niacin, vitamin E, preformed vitamin A) the UL applies only to supplements, fortified forms or preformed vitamin A, so food totals are not compared to it here.
      • CDRR (Chronic Disease Risk Reduction intake): for sodium, reducing intake above 2,300 mg/day is expected to lower chronic disease risk.
      • AMDR (Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution Range): the range of energy from protein, carbohydrate and fat linked to lower chronic disease risk with adequate nutrients.

      What 3–5 days can and cannot tell you

      • DRIs refer to usual intake over a long period. A few days of records will not match usual intake, especially for nutrients concentrated in a few foods (e.g., vitamin A, vitamin D, B12).
      • Being below the RDA does not mean you are deficient. Being below the EAR on usual intake means inadequacy is more likely. Diagnosing deficiency needs clinical and biochemical data.
      • Records are affected by under-reporting, portion estimation errors and changing what you eat because you are recording it.
      • Supplements are not included.

      Energy expenditure

      • Resting energy (basal energy expenditure) is estimated with the Mifflin-St Jeor equation from sex, age, height and weight.
      • Each activity has a MET value from the 2024 Adult Compendium of Physical Activities: how many times resting energy that activity uses. Energy for an activity = MET × resting energy per hour × hours.
      • Physical activity level (PAL) = total energy expenditure for the day ÷ resting energy. It is only calculated for days with a full 24 hours logged.
      • Your average PAL places you in one of four categories used by the 2023 Dietary Reference Intakes for energy: inactive (1.0 to under 1.53), low active (1.53 to under 1.68), active (1.68 to under 1.85) and very active (1.85 to under 2.5).
      • The Estimated Energy Requirement (EER) is then calculated with the 2023 equation for your sex and PAL category. It predicts the intake that keeps body weight stable; it is not a target for weight loss or gain.
      • Comparing your reported intake with your EER is a check on the food record. Intakes well below the EER are common because people tend to under-report what they eat.

      About the food data

      • Nutrient values come from Health Canada's Canadian Nutrient File (CNF), per 100 g of edible portion, scaled by the measure you choose.
      • CNF foods are mostly generic. Pick the closest match, or enter a mixed dish as its main ingredients.
      • Foods tagged Label are packaged products from Open Food Facts, a volunteer database built from product labels. They list only what is on the Nutrition Facts table (energy, fats, carbohydrate, sugars, fibre, protein, sodium, and sometimes potassium, calcium and iron), so most vitamins and minerals will show as missing. Entries can contain errors. Values for drinks are often per 100 mL; this tool treats 1 mL as 1 g.
      • Data coverage shows how much of your food (by weight) has a value for that nutrient. A blank in the database is not a zero. Low coverage means the total is probably an underestimate.
      • Energy is the CNF energy value (kcal). The energy split uses 4 kcal/g for protein and carbohydrate, 9 for fat and 7 for alcohol, and is scaled to 100%.
      • Total sugars include sugars naturally in foods. The CNF does not report added sugars.
